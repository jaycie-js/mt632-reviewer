ILO 5-1TOS 5.1 Muscle tissue

Identify the common properties of muscle tissue and the primary functions of skeletal muscle.

  • 4 properties: Contractility = shorten forcefully · Excitability = respond to a stimulus · Extensibility = stretched beyond resting length and still able to contract · Elasticity = recoil to original length U5 p5
  • 3 muscle types: Skeletal – attached to bones, striated, voluntary · Cardiac – heart, striated, involuntary · Smooth – vessels and hollow organs, non-striated, involuntary U5 p3
  • 7 functions: movement of the body · maintain posture · respiration · production of body heat · communication · constriction of organs and vessels · contraction of the heart U5 p4
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Smooth muscle: small spindle-shaped, usually one nucleus, myofilaments not organized into sarcomeres, slower than skeletal, no oxygen deficit, some autorhythmic U5 p89
  • Cardiac muscle: long, striated, branching, usually one nucleus; autorhythmic; intercalated disks (desmosomes + gap junctions); cells act as a single unit U5 p90

ILO 5-2TOS 5.1 Muscle tissue · Sarcomere

Recognize the characteristics of skeletal muscle fibers and the components of a sarcomere.

  • Coverings (outside → in): epimysium around the whole muscle → perimysium around each fascicle (bundle of fibers) → endomysium around each fiber. All three merge into tendons U5 p6
  • Fiber: very large multinucleated cell (nuclei just under the membrane), striated; fiber number is fixed after birth, so muscles enlarge by fiber size U5 p8
  • Electrical structures: sarcolemma (cell membrane) with inward T tubules → touching the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), whose enlarged ends are terminal cisternae (store Ca²⁺). Triad = 2 terminal cisternae + 1 T tubule. Cytoplasm = sarcoplasm U5 p9–10
  • Mechanical structures: myofibrils are bundles of filaments made of actin (thin) and myosin (thick) myofilaments arranged into repeating sarcomeres U5 p12
  • Sarcomere = basic structural and functional unit; extends Z disk → Z disk. Z disks anchor actin U5 p13
  • Bands: I band = light, actin only · A band = dark, length of the myosin · H zone = centre of A band, myosin only · M line = protein that holds myosin in place U5 p15
  • Thin filament = actin + troponin (binds Ca²⁺) + tropomyosin (blocks myosin binding sites). Myosin = 'golf club' heads with ATP-binding site, ATPase and actin attachment site → cross-bridges U5 p17
  • Sliding filament model: actin and myosin slide past each other, sarcomere shortens; the filaments themselves stay the same length; relaxation = sarcomere lengthens U5 p25–26
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Fiber length ranges up to 30 cm (Tier 3 number) U5 p8
  • T tubules occur at regular intervals and reach the centre of the fiber U5 p9

Skip unless time (Tier 3): Fiber length 1 mm–30 cm.

ILO 5-3TOS Neuromuscular junction

Identify the components of the neuromuscular junction.

  • NMJ = synapse where a motor neuron meets a muscle fiber. Motor unit = one motor neuron + all the fibers it stimulates U5 p21
  • Parts: presynaptic terminal (end of axon) · synaptic cleft (gap) · postsynaptic membrane = motor end-plate (the sarcolemma) · synaptic vesicles (store neurotransmitter) U5 p22
  • Neurotransmitter = acetylcholine (ACh): released from vesicles, diffuses across the cleft, binds ligand-gated Na⁺ channels in the sarcolemma U5 p23, p40
  • Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh into acetic acid and choline; the neuron re-absorbs choline to make more ACh U5 p41
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Fiber is innervated by a branch of a motor neuron; contact causes an action potential in the fiber U5 p39

ILO 5-4TOS Types of muscle contraction

Describe the mechanism responsible for the different amounts of tension produced in a muscle fiber.

  • Twitch = single contraction in response to one stimulus; 3 phases: lag (latent) → contraction → relaxation U5 p56
  • Graded response: force in a fiber depends on the number of cross-bridges formed (more = stronger). One factor is the frequency of stimulation U5 p61
  • Low frequency → twitches that fully relax. Higher frequency → fiber can't fully relax → more cross-bridges → summation, tension rises U5 p62
  • Incomplete tetanus = only partial relaxation between stimuli · Tetanus (complete) = sustained contraction, no relaxation U5 p63
  • Recruitment = stimulating more motor units (whole-muscle force); summation = single fiber U5 p60, p63
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Muscle tone = constant tension over long periods; a small % of motor units contract tetanically, out of phase with each other (keeps back/legs straight, head up) U5 p66

ILO 5-5TOS Muscle levers

Describe the classes of levers and explain how they make muscles more efficient.

  • Lever = rigid structure moving on a fixed point (fulcrum). Bones = levers · joints = fulcrums · muscles supply the applied force (AF) to move a load U5 p93
  • Levers can change the direction of AF, the distance and speed produced, and the effective strength of AF U5 p94
  • 1st class: fulcrum between AF and load (pry bar/crowbar). Example: extending the neck, lifting the head U5 p95
  • 2nd class: load between AF and fulcrum (wheelbarrow); a small force moves a large weight. Example: plantar flexion by calf muscles U5 p95
  • 3rd class: AF between load and fulcrum (tongs); most common in the body; maximizes speed and distance at the expense of force. Example: elbow flexion by biceps brachii U5 p98
  • Memory aid: 1st = F in the middle · 2nd = L in the middle · 3rd = AF in the middle (derived from the three definitions)

ILO 5-6TOS Excitation–contraction coupling · Contraction cycle

Summarize the events involved in the neural control of skeletal muscle contraction and relaxation.

  • Action potential (AP) phases: resting (gated channels closed) → depolarization (Na⁺ enters; threshold triggers AP) → repolarization (Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open, K⁺ exits); Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores resting state U5 p34–35
  • At the NMJ: ① AP reaches terminal → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open ② Ca²⁺ → vesicles release ACh (exocytosis) ③ ACh binds ligand-gated Na⁺ channels ④ Na⁺ enters → threshold → AP in the muscle fiber ⑤ ACh unbinds, channels close ⑥ acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh down U5 p40–41, p43
  • Excitation–contraction coupling: AP travels along sarcolemma and down T tubules → opens gated Ca²⁺ channels in terminal cisternae of the SR → Ca²⁺ released into sarcoplasm U5 p44
  • Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → myosin binding sites exposed → myosin heads bind actin; ATP breakdown (ADP + Pi) moves the myosin heads and the cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ is present U5 p45
  • Cross-bridge cycle (6 steps): 1 exposure of active sites · 2 cross-bridge formation · 3 power stroke (head bends, pulls actin toward the H zone) · 4 release (ATP binds the head) · 5 ATP hydrolysis (ADP + Pi stay on head) · 6 recovery stroke (head returns, binds farther along actin) U5 p50–51
  • Relaxation: ACh no longer released → APs to SR stop → Ca²⁺ actively pumped back into SR (uses ATP) → tropomyosin blocks sites again → cross-bridge cycle stops U5 p54
  • ATP is needed for: detaching the myosin head, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, and the Ca²⁺ pump into the SR U5 p55
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Resting potential (inside negative; K⁺ leak, Na⁺/K⁺ pump) is the same mechanism described in Unit 6 U5 p27–32
  • Energy from one ATP per cross-bridge cycle; heads are at different stages so some always stay attached U5 p49

ILO 5-7TOS Types of muscle contraction

ILO 5-8TOS Energy for contraction

Recall the mechanisms by which muscle fibers obtain the energy to power contractions.

  • Order of ATP supply as exercise starts: ① small stored ATP in the fiber → ② adenylate kinase + creatine kinase pathways → ③ anaerobic respiration (fast-twitch) → ④ aerobic respiration U5 p84
  • Adenylate kinase: 2 ADP → 1 ATP + 1 AMP. Creatine kinase: transfers a phosphate from creatine phosphate to ADP → ATP U5 p77
  • Anaerobic: glycolysis: 1 glucose → 2 pyruvate (→ lactate) + 2 ATP; fast-twitch fibers; short-term. The lactate is passed to slow-twitch fibers as a start for aerobic ATP U5 p80
  • Aerobic: needs O₂; 1 glucose → up to 36 ATP + H₂O + CO₂; ATP for hours U5 p82
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Three ATP-dependent proteins: myosin head, Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, Ca²⁺ pump in SR U5 p76
  • Fatigue = temporary reduced work capacity; mechanisms: acidosis + ATP depletion, oxidative stress (ROS), local inflammation U5 p85–87. Lactate + protons lower pH → less effective Ca²⁺ U5 p86
  • Physiological contracture = not enough ATP to bind myosin → can neither contract nor relax U5 p87; Psychological fatigue = mind perceives contraction impossible U5 p87
  • Oxygen deficit = lag before breathing increases at exercise onset; excess post-exercise O₂ consumption = lag before breathing returns to normal U5 p88

Skip unless time (Tier 3): Time numbers: 5–6 s stored ATP, ~15 s kinase pathways, 30–40 s anaerobic (appear on p76–84 but are numbers).

ILO 5-9TOS Types of muscle fiber

Identify the types of muscle fibers based on muscle performance.

  • SO – slow-twitch oxidative (Type I): contracts slowly, fatigues slowly, lots of myoglobin and mitochondria, aerobic, dark/red, good blood supply, low glycogen. Long-distance runners U5 p69, p72
  • FOG – fast-twitch oxidative glycolytic (Type IIa): high myoglobin, many mitochondria, intermediate fatigue resistance, fast ATP breakdown, high glycogen, endurance activities, lower limbs U5 p71–72
  • FG – fast-twitch glycolytic (Type IIx): contracts and fatigues quickly, low myoglobin, few mitochondria, anaerobic, light colour, high glycogen. Sprinters U5 p70, p72
  • Distribution: most muscles are a blend. Large postural muscles (back, lower limbs) have more slow-twitch; upper-limb muscles have more fast-twitch U5 p73

ILO 5-10TOS Hypertrophy, atrophy, aging · 5.3 Diseases

Explain muscle hypertrophy, atrophy, and aging and how physical conditioning affects muscle tissue.

  • Hypertrophy = fibers get bigger (not more numerous) → more strength and endurance; new nuclei from satellite cells; more blood vessels, connective tissue, mitochondria U5 p75
  • Atrophy = an unused muscle decreases in size U5 p75
  • Training: neither fiber type easily converts to the other; training enlarges both. Intense (anaerobic) exercise → strength and mass, fast-twitch enlarge more. Aerobic exercise → vascularity, slow-twitch enlarge more. Athletic training also increases the number of motor units recruited at once U5 p74
  • Aging: reduced muscle mass · slower response time · reduced stamina · longer recovery · smaller NMJ surface area · lower capillary density U5 p91

  • 5.3 – Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Fig 7.26): symptoms muscle weakness, muscle atrophy, contractures; treatment = physical therapy to prevent contractures; no effective treatment to prevent atrophy; also affects respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive, urinary, nervous systems U5 p92
  • 5.3 – Tetanus (the disease), shown only as images: rigid body posture, clenched jaw (lockjaw), muscle stiffness or spasms, opisthotonos. Not the same as tetanus the contraction state U5 p65

ILO 6-1TOS 6.1 Major divisions and subdivisions

Recognize the anatomical and functional divisions of the nervous system.

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord. PNS = all nervous tissue outside the CNS U6a1 s5
  • PNS sensory (afferent) division → receptors to CNS (sensory neurons). PNS motor (efferent) division → CNS to effectors (muscles, glands) U6a1 s5
  • Motor division splits into: somatic NS (→ skeletal muscle) · autonomic NS (→ cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, glands; sympathetic + parasympathetic) · enteric NS (digestive tract only) U6a1 s6
  • Functions of the NS: receive sensory input · integrate information · control muscles and glands · maintain homeostasis · establish mental activity U6a1 s4
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Neurons receive stimuli and conduct APs; glial cells support and do NOT conduct APs U6a1 s8
  • Neuron = cell body + dendrites (receive) + axon (leaves at the axon hillock). Types: multipolar (most CNS neurons, nearly all motor), bipolar (retina, nasal cavity), pseudo-unipolar (most sensory) U6a1 s9–12
  • Glia: astrocytes (major CNS support; blood-brain barrier) · ependymal (line CSF cavities) · microglia (immune; remove debris) · oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin) · Schwann cells (PNS myelin) · satellite cells (support PNS cell bodies) U6a1 s14–15
  • Gray matter = cell bodies + dendrites (clusters: nuclei in CNS, ganglia in PNS). White matter = myelinated axons (tracts in CNS, nerves in PNS) U6a1 s21–22

ILO 6-2TOS 6.1 Synapse

Recall the structure of a synapse and the mechanism involved in synaptic activity.

  • Structure: presynaptic terminal (axon end) · synaptic cleft · postsynaptic membrane; neurotransmitters (NTs) stored in synaptic vesicles U6a1 s47
  • Steps: ① AP reaches terminal → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ enters ② Ca²⁺ → NT released by exocytosis ③ NT diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors ④ chemically gated channels open or close on the postsynaptic membrane U6a1 s48–49
  • Result depends on the channel: Na⁺ opens → depolarization (AP if threshold) · K⁺ or Cl⁻ opens → hyperpolarization (AP inhibited) U6a1 s50
  • Best-known NTs: acetylcholine and norepinephrine U6a1 s50
  • Ending the signal: acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh; norepinephrine is taken back into the presynaptic terminal or broken down by enzymes U6a1 s51
  • Pathways: converging = 2+ neurons onto one postsynaptic neuron · diverging = one axon branches to several neurons U6a1 s53–55
  • Summation (one input rarely reaches threshold): spatial = inputs from different locations (e.g., converging) · temporal = inputs overlapping in time (one input firing rapidly) U6a1 s56–57

ILO 6-3TOS 6.1 Resting membrane potential

Explain the mechanism of resting membrane potential.

  • Membrane is polarized: inside negative, outside positive; this small voltage at rest = resting membrane potential (RMP) U6a1 s25
  • Channels: leak channels always open · gated channels closed until a signal: chemically gated (opened by NTs) or voltage-gated (opened by a change in membrane voltage) U6a1 s23–24
  • Factor 1 – uneven distribution: K⁺ higher inside, Na⁺ higher outside; large negatively charged proteins trapped inside U6a1 s26 U5 p30
  • Factor 2 – leak channels: many more K⁺ leak channels than Na⁺ → membrane far more permeable to K⁺ → K⁺ leaves → inside becomes more negative U6a1 s28 U5 p31
  • Factor 3 – Na⁺/K⁺ pump: uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, maintaining the gradients U6a1 s30 U5 p31
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • The pump uses ~25% of a typical cell's ATP and ~70% of a neuron's U6a1 s30

Skip unless time (Tier 3): Exact '50–100×' leak-channel ratio and the ATP percentages (single-slide numbers).

ILO 6-4TOS 6.1 Action potential

Recall the events involved in generating and propagating an action potential and the factors affecting it.

  • At rest: voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are closed U6a1 s34
  • Depolarization: stimulus → chemically gated channels → Na⁺ enters (local current). If too weak, the local potential just disappears. If it reaches threshold → voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (generally at the axon hillock) → rapid Na⁺ entry, inside becomes positive U6a1 s34–37
  • Repolarization: Na⁺ channels close, voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, K⁺ leaves → inside negative again U6a1 s37
  • Hyperpolarization = brief overshoot below RMP; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump helps restore resting conditions U6a1 s38
  • All-or-none: threshold reached → AP occurs; not reached → no AP U6a1 s38
  • Propagation: unmyelinated axon = continuous conduction along the whole membrane (slower) · myelinated axon = saltatory conduction, jumping between nodes of Ranvier (faster) U6a1 s44
  • Factors affecting AP: reaching threshold (stimulus strength) · myelination (increases speed/efficiency) · axon diameter (larger-diameter, heavily myelinated axons conduct fastest; medium, lightly myelinated autonomic axons are slower) U6a1 s18, s46
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Myelin = insulating layers: oligodendrocytes (CNS), Schwann cells (PNS); nodes of Ranvier = gaps where ions move. Multiple sclerosis = disease of the myelin sheath, loss of muscle function U6a1 s17–18
  • Fast pain = myelinated Type A fibers · slow pain = unmyelinated Type C fibers U6b p22–23

Skip unless time (Tier 3): Speeds (3–15 m/s vs 15–120 m/s) and '600-fold increase'; AP lasts 1–3 ms (U5 p33).

ILO 6-5TOS 6.2 Sensory pathways

Identify the components of the afferent and efferent divisions of the nervous system.

  • Afferent division = somatic and visceral sensory pathways. Somatic sensory information goes to the cerebral cortex; visceral sensory information goes mainly to the brainstem and diencephalon U6b p5
  • Efferent division = motor pathways to peripheral effectors (somatic motor → skeletal muscle). Note U6a1 s5–6 also counts autonomic motor neurons to cardiac/smooth muscle and glands as efferent U6b p5 U6a1 s5–6
  • 3-neuron sensory chain: 1st-order (receptor → CNS; cell body in a spinal/cranial ganglion) → 2nd-order (interneuron in spinal cord/brainstem; crosses to opposite side = decussation) → 3rd-order (thalamus) → primary somatosensory cortex U6b p38–39
  • Spinothalamic: crude touch, pressure, pain, temperature (anterior = crude touch/pressure; lateral = pain/temperature); 2nd-order neurons cross in the spinal cord U6b p42–43
  • Posterior column: fine touch, vibration, pressure, proprioception; gracile (lower body) + cuneate (upper body) fasciculi; 2nd-order neurons synapse in the medulla and cross there (medial lemniscus) U6b p48–49
  • Spinocerebellar: position of muscles, tendons, joints → cerebellum; does not reach awareness U6b p52–53
  • Visceral sensory: interoceptors in thoracic/abdominopelvic organs; 2nd-order neurons ascend in the spinothalamic pathway to the solitary nuclei of the medulla (→ cardiovascular/respiratory centres) U6b p55
  • Ascending tracts cross: left brain receives input from the right body (and vice versa) U6a2 s5
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Referred pain = visceral pain felt on the body surface (same spinal segment), e.g. heart attack in left arm. Phantom limb = pain in an amputated limb because the labeled line is still active U6b p46–47
  • Sensory homunculus = map of somatosensory cortex; area ∝ density of sensory neurons, not body size (lips/tongue large) U6b p51
  • 6.2 note: Sensory = ascending tracts ('spino-' prefix): spinothalamic, dorsal column, spinocerebellar U6a2 s4–6

ILO 6-6TOS 6.2 Receptors

Recognize receptor response to different specific stimuli.

  • Sensation = information arriving in the CNS · Perception = conscious awareness of a sensation · Transduction = receptor converts the stimulus into an AP U6b p7
  • General senses: temperature, pain, touch, pressure, vibration, proprioception · Special senses: smell, taste, vision, equilibrium, hearing U6b p8
  • Receptor specificity: each receptor responds to a characteristic stimulus. Receptive field = area monitored by one receptor; larger field → harder to localize the stimulus U6b p9
  • Receptor potential changes membrane potential; depolarizing = generator potential (toward threshold); size ∝ stimulus strength. Labeled line: a receptor's pathway determines what is perceived U6b p11–13
  • Tonic receptors: always active; stimulation changes AP frequency · Phasic receptors: normally inactive; burst of APs when the stimulus changes U6b p14
  • Adaptation = reduced sensitivity to a constant stimulus. Fast-adapting = phasic (e.g., temperature); slow-adapting = tonic (e.g., pain) U6b p17
  • By location: exteroceptors (outside world) · proprioceptors (muscle/joint position) · interoceptors (visceral organs) U6b p19
  • By stimulus: nociceptors = pain · thermoreceptors = temperature · mechanoreceptors = physical distortion (touch, pressure, vibration; includes tactile receptors, baroreceptors, proprioceptors) · chemoreceptors = chemical concentration U6b p20, p25
More detail (Tier 2, supporting)
  • Nociceptors = free nerve endings, large receptive fields, tonic; endorphins/enkephalins inhibit pain pathways U6b p21. Fast pain = Type A (myelinated), prickling; slow pain = Type C (unmyelinated), burning/aching U6b p22–23
  • Thermoreceptors = free nerve endings (dermis, skeletal muscle, liver, hypothalamus), phasic U6b p24
  • Tactile receptors (skin): free nerve endings · root hair plexus · tactile discs (fine touch) · bulbous (Ruffini) corpuscles · lamellar (Pacinian) = fast-adapting, deep pressure/high-frequency vibration · tactile (Meissner) = fine touch, low-frequency vibration U6b p27–32
  • Baroreceptors = pressure change in vessel/organ walls U6b p33. Proprioceptors: muscle spindles (length), Golgi tendon organs (tension), joint-capsule receptors U6b p35
  • Chemoreceptors: carotid bodies and aortic bodies; monitor pH, CO₂, O₂ in arterial blood U6b p36

ILO 6-7TOS 6.2 Somatic motor pathway

Identify the components, processes, and functions of the somatic motor pathways, and the levels of information processing involved in motor control.

  • Somatic NS controls skeletal muscle. A somatic motor pathway always has ≥ 2 motor neurons: upper motor neuron (cell body in a CNS centre: primary motor or premotor cortex) → lower motor neuron (cell body in a brainstem nucleus or spinal cord anterior horn; axon leaves the CNS to a motor unit) U6b p57 U6a2 s10–11
  • 3 pathways: corticospinal · medial · lateral. They carry conscious and subconscious commands; basal nuclei and cerebellum monitor and adjust them U6b p58
  • Corticospinal (pyramidal): voluntary control of skeletal muscle. Corticobulbar → cranial-nerve motor nuclei (eyes, jaw, face, some neck/pharynx). Lateral corticospinal – most axons decussate at the pyramids of the medulla. Anterior corticospinal – cross at the level of the lower motor neuron U6b p60–62, p70
  • Medial pathway → muscle tone and gross movements of trunk and proximal limbs; posture and balance. Lateral pathway → distal limb muscles for precise movement U6b p65
  • Levels of control: cerebral cortex (conscious) → brainstem centres (subconscious: medial/lateral) → basal nuclei (background patterns of voluntary movement) and cerebellum (compares position/visual/balance input, adjusts upper motor neurons, inhibits unnecessary commands; learned by trial and error) U6b p58, p71–72
  • Motor homunculus: area size ∝ degree of fine motor control (hands, face, tongue large; trunk small) U6b p64

ILO 7-1TOS 7.1 Principles of chemical communication

Compare the modes of intercellular communication that occur in the endocrine and nervous systems.

  • Endocrine system = glands and endocrine cells that release tiny amounts of hormones into the bloodstream (not a duct); hormones travel in general circulation to target tissues that have the specific receptor U7 s3

ILO 7-3TOS 7.1 Mechanisms of hormone action

Recall the general mechanisms of hormonal action on target organs.

ILO 5-1

Identify the common properties of muscle tissue and the primary functions of skeletal muscle.

  • 4 properties: Contractility = shorten forcefully · Excitability = respond to a stimulus · Extensibility = stretched beyond resting length and still able to contract · Elasticity = recoil to original length U5 p5

ILO 5-2

Recognize the characteristics of skeletal muscle fibers and the components of a sarcomere.

  • Coverings (outside → in): epimysium around the whole muscle → perimysium around each fascicle (bundle of fibers) → endomysium around each fiber. All three merge into tendons U5 p6
  • Fiber: very large multinucleated cell (nuclei just under the membrane), striated; fiber number is fixed after birth, so muscles enlarge by fiber size U5 p8
  • Electrical structures: sarcolemma (cell membrane) with inward T tubules → touching the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), whose enlarged ends are terminal cisternae (store Ca²⁺). Triad = 2 terminal cisternae + 1 T tubule. Cytoplasm = sarcoplasm U5 p9–10
  • Mechanical structures: myofibrils are bundles of filaments made of actin (thin) and myosin (thick) myofilaments arranged into repeating sarcomeres U5 p12
  • Sarcomere = basic structural and functional unit; extends Z disk → Z disk. Z disks anchor actin U5 p13
  • Bands: I band = light, actin only · A band = dark, length of the myosin · H zone = centre of A band, myosin only · M line = protein that holds myosin in place U5 p15
  • Thin filament = actin + troponin (binds Ca²⁺) + tropomyosin (blocks myosin binding sites). Myosin = 'golf club' heads with ATP-binding site, ATPase and actin attachment site → cross-bridges U5 p17
  • Sliding filament model: actin and myosin slide past each other, sarcomere shortens; the filaments themselves stay the same length; relaxation = sarcomere lengthens U5 p25–26
ILO 5-3

Identify the components of the neuromuscular junction.

  • NMJ = synapse where a motor neuron meets a muscle fiber. Motor unit = one motor neuron + all the fibers it stimulates U5 p21
  • Parts: presynaptic terminal (end of axon) · synaptic cleft (gap) · postsynaptic membrane = motor end-plate (the sarcolemma) · synaptic vesicles (store neurotransmitter) U5 p22
  • Neurotransmitter = acetylcholine (ACh): released from vesicles, diffuses across the cleft, binds ligand-gated Na⁺ channels in the sarcolemma U5 p23, p40
  • Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh into acetic acid and choline; the neuron re-absorbs choline to make more ACh U5 p41
ILO 5-4

Describe the mechanism responsible for the different amounts of tension produced in a muscle fiber.

  • Twitch = single contraction in response to one stimulus; 3 phases: lag (latent) → contraction → relaxation U5 p56
  • Graded response: force in a fiber depends on the number of cross-bridges formed (more = stronger). One factor is the frequency of stimulation U5 p61
  • Low frequency → twitches that fully relax. Higher frequency → fiber can't fully relax → more cross-bridges → summation, tension rises U5 p62
  • Incomplete tetanus = only partial relaxation between stimuli · Tetanus (complete) = sustained contraction, no relaxation U5 p63
  • Recruitment = stimulating more motor units (whole-muscle force); summation = single fiber U5 p60, p63
ILO 5-5

Describe the classes of levers and explain how they make muscles more efficient.

  • Lever = rigid structure moving on a fixed point (fulcrum). Bones = levers · joints = fulcrums · muscles supply the applied force (AF) to move a load U5 p93
  • Levers can change the direction of AF, the distance and speed produced, and the effective strength of AF U5 p94
  • 1st class: fulcrum between AF and load (pry bar/crowbar). Example: extending the neck, lifting the head U5 p95
  • 2nd class: load between AF and fulcrum (wheelbarrow); a small force moves a large weight. Example: plantar flexion by calf muscles U5 p95
  • 3rd class: AF between load and fulcrum (tongs); most common in the body; maximizes speed and distance at the expense of force. Example: elbow flexion by biceps brachii U5 p98
  • Memory aid: 1st = F in the middle · 2nd = L in the middle · 3rd = AF in the middle (derived from the three definitions)
ILO 5-6

Summarize the events involved in the neural control of skeletal muscle contraction and relaxation.

  • Action potential (AP) phases: resting (gated channels closed) → depolarization (Na⁺ enters; threshold triggers AP) → repolarization (Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open, K⁺ exits); Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores resting state U5 p34–35

  • Excitation–contraction coupling: AP travels along sarcolemma and down T tubules → opens gated Ca²⁺ channels in terminal cisternae of the SR → Ca²⁺ released into sarcoplasm U5 p44
  • Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → myosin binding sites exposed → myosin heads bind actin; ATP breakdown (ADP + Pi) moves the myosin heads and the cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ is present U5 p45
  • Cross-bridge cycle (6 steps): 1 exposure of active sites · 2 cross-bridge formation · 3 power stroke (head bends, pulls actin toward the H zone) · 4 release (ATP binds the head) · 5 ATP hydrolysis (ADP + Pi stay on head) · 6 recovery stroke (head returns, binds farther along actin) U5 p50–51
  • Relaxation: ACh no longer released → APs to SR stop → Ca²⁺ actively pumped back into SR (uses ATP) → tropomyosin blocks sites again → cross-bridge cycle stops U5 p54
  • ATP is needed for: detaching the myosin head, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, and the Ca²⁺ pump into the SR U5 p55

Recall the mechanisms by which muscle fibers obtain the energy to power contractions.

  • Order of ATP supply as exercise starts: ① small stored ATP in the fiber → ② adenylate kinase + creatine kinase pathways → ③ anaerobic respiration (fast-twitch) → ④ aerobic respiration U5 p84

Excitation–contraction coupling (nerve → contraction)

  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters the terminal
  3. Ca²⁺ → synaptic vesicles release ACh (exocytosis)
  4. ACh binds ligand-gated Na⁺ channels on the sarcolemma (motor end-plate)
  5. Na⁺ enters → threshold → AP in the muscle fiber
  6. AP travels along the sarcolemma and down the T tubules
  7. Terminal cisternae of the SR open Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ released into the sarcoplasm

Cross-bridge cycle (sliding filament)
  1. Active sites exposed (Ca²⁺ on troponin; tropomyosin moves)
  2. Cross-bridge formation: myosin head binds actin
  3. Power stroke: head bends, pulls actin toward the H zone → sarcomere shortens
  4. Release: a new ATP binds the head and it detaches
  5. ATP hydrolysis: ADP + Pi stay on the head (energy stored)
  6. Recovery stroke: head returns, binds farther along actin; repeats while Ca²⁺ is present

Relaxation

  2. APs to the SR stop
  3. Ca²⁺ pumped back into the SR (uses ATP)
  4. Ca²⁺ leaves troponin; tropomyosin blocks actin sites again
  5. Cross-bridge cycle stops → muscle relaxes

Recall the structure of a synapse and the mechanism involved in synaptic activity.

ILO 6-3

Explain the mechanism of resting membrane potential.

  • Membrane is polarized: inside negative, outside positive; this small voltage at rest = resting membrane potential (RMP) U6a1 s25
  • Channels: leak channels always open · gated channels closed until a signal: chemically gated (opened by NTs) or voltage-gated (opened by a change in membrane voltage) U6a1 s23–24
  • Factor 1 – uneven distribution: K⁺ higher inside, Na⁺ higher outside; large negatively charged proteins trapped inside U6a1 s26 U5 p30
  • Factor 2 – leak channels: many more K⁺ leak channels than Na⁺ → membrane far more permeable to K⁺ → K⁺ leaves → inside becomes more negative U6a1 s28 U5 p31
  • Factor 3 – Na⁺/K⁺ pump: uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, maintaining the gradients U6a1 s30 U5 p31
ILO 6-4

Recall the events involved in generating and propagating an action potential and the factors affecting it.

  • At rest: voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are closed U6a1 s34
  • Depolarization: stimulus → chemically gated channels → Na⁺ enters (local current). If too weak, the local potential just disappears. If it reaches threshold → voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (generally at the axon hillock) → rapid Na⁺ entry, inside becomes positive U6a1 s34–37
  • Repolarization: Na⁺ channels close, voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, K⁺ leaves → inside negative again U6a1 s37
  • Hyperpolarization = brief overshoot below RMP; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump helps restore resting conditions U6a1 s38
  • All-or-none: threshold reached → AP occurs; not reached → no AP U6a1 s38
  • Propagation: unmyelinated axon = continuous conduction along the whole membrane (slower) · myelinated axon = saltatory conduction, jumping between nodes of Ranvier (faster) U6a1 s44
  • Factors affecting AP: reaching threshold (stimulus strength) · myelination (increases speed/efficiency) · axon diameter (larger-diameter, heavily myelinated axons conduct fastest; medium, lightly myelinated autonomic axons are slower) U6a1 s18, s46

  • Endocrine system = glands and endocrine cells that release tiny amounts of hormones into the bloodstream (not a duct); hormones travel in general circulation to target tissues that have the specific receptor U7 s3

  • Paracrine: affects other cell types nearby (e.g., histamine → vasodilation of nearby vessels) U7 s5
  • Neurotransmitter / neuromodulator: secreted by a nerve cell into a synaptic cleft to activate an adjacent cell (e.g., acetylcholine) U7 s6
  • Endocrine (hormone): secreted into the blood, binds receptors on distant target tissues (e.g., epinephrine, insulin) U7 s6
  • Compare: nervous system = signal across a synapse to one adjacent cell; endocrine = signal via blood to any cell with the receptor. [Inference summarizing s3, s6]
ILO 7-2

Compare the major structural classes of hormones.

  • Water-soluble: proteins, peptides, amino acids; the most common type (e.g., growth hormone, ADH, prolactin) U7 s9
  • Lipid-soluble: steroids and eicosanoids (e.g., androgens); thyroid hormones and steroid hormones generally bind nuclear receptors U7 s9, s27
  • Transport: lipid-soluble hormones ride bound to binding proteins (protected from enzymes/kidney), last days–weeks · water-soluble hormones mostly travel free, short half-lives U7 s10–12
  • Receptors: lipid-soluble (small, cross the membrane) → nuclear receptors · water-soluble (polar, cannot cross) → membrane-bound receptors U7 s25–28
ILO 7-3

Recall the general mechanisms of hormonal action on target organs.

  • Specificity: a hormone affects only cells with its receptor (receptor site matches by shape/chemistry) U7 s23–24
  • Lipid-soluble (nuclear receptor) pathway: hormone enters the cell → binds receptor in cytoplasm/nucleus → hormone–receptor complex binds hormone-response element on DNA → transcribes mRNA → ribosomes make new proteins = response. Takes hours. (thyroid and steroid hormones) U7 s27, s29–30
  • Water-soluble (membrane receptor) pathway: hormone binds membrane receptor → receptor changes shape and activates a G protein (GDP → GTP on the α subunit) → α subunit activates adenylate cyclase → ATP → cAMP (second messenger) → cAMP activates protein kinases → cell response. Phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down and stops stimulation U7 s32–36
  • Amplification: each receptor produces thousands of second messengers → huge final response U7 s38
  • Control of release: humoral (blood chemical, e.g. low blood Ca²⁺), neural (neurons stimulate), hormonal (one hormone stimulates another). The same three stimuli can stimulate or inhibit U7 s14–17
  • Feedback: most hormones use negative feedback (hormone inhibits its own secretion once levels are high); some use positive feedback (tropic hormone action; oxytocin) U7 s21, s57
ILO 7-4

Identify the location, hormones, and functions of the endocrine glands.

  • Pituitary (hypophysis): small gland in the brain controlled by the hypothalamus; anterior = epithelial tissue, posterior = neural tissue (extension of the brain) U7 s40
  • Hypothalamus → anterior pituitary: releasing and inhibiting hormones travel in the hypothalamic–pituitary portal system U7 s42. Posterior pituitary stores/releases hormones made in the hypothalamus (ADH and oxytocin) via the hypothalamic–hypophyseal tract U7 s44
  • Anterior pituitary: GH (growth of bone, muscle, organs; via IGFs from liver) · TSH (thyroid) · ACTH (adrenal cortex → cortisol) · LH (ovary: ovulation, estrogen/progesterone; testes: testosterone) · FSH (ovary follicle maturation; testes sperm production) · prolactin (mammary glands: milk production) U7 s46–55
  • Posterior pituitary: ADH (kidney tubules: conserve water; constrict vessels) · oxytocin (uterine contractions in labor; milk letdown) U7 s56–57
  • Thyroid: needs iodine; follicular cells make T3/T4 (regulate metabolic rate, growth); parafollicular cells make calcitonin (↓ blood Ca²⁺ by inhibiting osteoclasts). Axis: hypothalamus TRH → anterior pituitary TSH → thyroid T3/T4; T3/T4 inhibit TRH and TSH (negative feedback) U7 s58–65, s61
  • Parathyroid: PTH raises blood Ca²⁺ (more than calcitonin lowers it): osteoclasts break down bone, ↑ intestinal absorption, ↓ urinary loss; ↑ active vitamin D U7 s66–67
  • Adrenal medulla: epinephrine / norepinephrine – fight-or-flight: ↑ glucose and fatty acids, ↑ HR/BP, ↑ blood flow to muscle, reinforce sympathetic effects U7 s70–71
  • Adrenal cortex: aldosterone (mineralocorticoid; zona glomerulosa; kidneys retain Na⁺ and water, secrete K⁺) · cortisol (glucocorticoid; zona fasciculata; stress, ↑ glucose, ↓ inflammation) · androgens (zona reticularis) U7 s73–77. Axis: CRH → ACTH → cortisol, negative feedback U7 s76
  • Pancreas (mixed gland; islets of Langerhans): α cells → glucagon · β cells → insulin · δ cells → somatostatin U7 s78–79. Insulin: after a meal, ↓ blood glucose, stores glycogen. Glucagon: between meals, liver breaks glycogen to glucose, ↑ glucose U7 s81, s83
  • Gonads: testes → testosterone; ovaries → estrogen/progesterone U7 s86–87
ILO 7-5

Explain the effects of variation in hormone production.

  • GH: too much in growth years → giantism; too little in growth years → pituitary dwarfism; too much after growth → acromegaly U7 s46
  • TSH: too much → thyroid enlarges (goiter); too little → thyroid shrinks U7 s49
  • ADH (low) → diabetes insipidus: large amounts of dilute urine → dehydration and thirst U7 s56
  • Hypothyroidism: ↓ metabolism, weight gain, fatigue, low temperature/pulse, dry cold skin; myxedema (adults), cretinism (infants) · Hyperthyroidism: ↑ metabolism, weight loss, nervousness, high temperature/pulse, warm flushed skin; Graves' disease → exophthalmia U7 s63
  • Cortisol: Cushing disease = excess; Addison disease = significant reduction U7 s74
  • Diabetes mellitus: too little insulin or faulty insulin receptors → hyperglycemia, exaggerated appetite, excess urine, dehydration, thirst, fatigue. Type I = insulin dependent (daily injections); Type II = insulin independent, often obese, can be treated with diet U7 s82
ILO 7-6

Explain how aging affects hormone production.

  • Gradual decrease in secretion of some hormones (some decreases may reflect less physical activity) U7 s92
  • ↓ GH → ↓ bone and muscle mass (can be offset with exercise) · ↓ melatonin → sleep-pattern changes · ↓ thyroid hormones · ↓ renin → less ability to respond to BP changes · ↓ reproductive hormones · ↓ thymosin → less effective immune system U7 s92
  • PTH increases (calcium and vitamin D drop) → loss of bone matrix U7 s93
  • Glucose regulation does not decline with age, but age-related type 2 diabetes tends to develop in people with a familial tendency U7 s93
